Temperament and Character Traits of Patients Hospitalized in a Forensic Psychiatry Unit in a Controlled Study

Abstract
Violent behavior may occur in patients with psychiatric disorders despite improvements in medication adherence and access to rehabilitation. Previous studies have extensively examined the factors underlying this behavior; however, the precise determinants of offending remain unclear. Certain personality disorders are thought to increase offending behavior. Temperament and character traits are known to play an important role in the dynamic interactions between individuals and their environment. This study aimed to identify the temperament and character traits of patients hospitalized in a forensic psychiatry unit and compare them with those of psychiatric outpatients with similar disorders and a control group.
The study was conducted in the High-Security Forensic Psychiatry (YGAP) Unit and the Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of Adana City Training and Research Hospital. Participants comprised 91 psychiatric patients (YGAP group, n = 66; outpatient group, n = 25) and 24 volunteers serving as controls. Sociodemographic data were collected using a semi-structured sociodemographic questionnaire. Temperament and character traits were assessed using the Turkish version of the Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A).
In the YGAP group, 29.4% of patients were diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia, 51.5% with other schizophrenia subtypes or atypical psychosis, and 19.1% with bipolar affective disorder. The corresponding proportions in the outpatient group were 18.4%, 52.3%, and 29.4%, respectively. Statistically significant differences were found between the patient groups and controls in marital status, employment, and social security coverage (χ² = 30.88, p < 0.000; χ² = 15.2, p < 0.000; and χ² = 17.18, p < 0.000, respectively). The patient groups differed from controls on all temperament subscales except hyperthymic temperament. The YGAP and outpatient groups did not differ in cyclothymic, hyperthymic, or anxious temperament, whereas differences in depressive and irritable temperament were statistically significant (SE = 0.951, p = 0.020; SE = 0.978, p = 0.002, respectively). Irritable temperament scores were higher in the YGAP group.
Numerous studies have reported higher levels of irritable temperament traits in patients with psychotic symptoms. Although our findings do not establish whether innate traits trigger offending behavior, they suggest that temperament and character may influence the course of illness.
Keywords: Violent behavior; temperament and character; forensic psychiatry
Introduction
The relationship between mental illness and violence has been examined in many studies, but its underlying causes remain unclear. Earlier studies suggested a two- to fourfold higher propensity for violence among individuals with mental disorders. More recent studies, however, have reported that their propensity to offend does not differ from that of the general population (Gkotsi, 2016; Ayar, 2021). This may be explained by improvements in medication adherence and access to psychiatric rehabilitation. Co-occurring alcohol or substance use, treatment nonadherence, lack of insight, previous offending, and certain personality traits are also considered factors that may increase offending behavior. Characteristics identified in studies of offending include being unmarried, male sex, limited social support, personality disorders, a history of offending, and alcohol or substance use (Bailey and MacCulloch, 1992; Hawang and Segal, 1996; Türkcan et al., 1997). Male sex and younger age are considered important risk factors for offending. Males are reported to be approximately ten times more likely to offend (Ong et al., 2009; İnan, 2018).
The circumstances or events under which patients exhibit violent tendencies may depend substantially on their temperament. Temperament determines innate, highly individual emotional responses. Character comprises acquired behavioral traits that develop on the basis of temperament and are relatively resistant to change. These traits are shaped by dynamic interactions between the individual and the environment. Temperament and character are thought to be important in tendencies toward violence. Although offending has often been associated with antisocial personality disorder, it also occurs in other personality disorders. Temperament and character traits have been reported to play a role in these tendencies (Martínez-López, 2019). One study suggested that narcissistic personality, described as a cynical character that regards others as self-interested, might predict offending. However, although this characteristic was significantly associated with criminality, it was not associated with the total number of arrests (Ameri, 2019). Specific risk factors for violence have been identified, and prevention efforts have focused on individuals with these factors. Many authors consider assessment of dangerousness and the risk of reoffending an integral component of every criminal justice system (Aydoğdu, 2024).
In Türkiye, individuals who are not criminally responsible because of psychiatric illness require care within forensic psychiatric services under criminal law. In this context, High-Security Forensic Psychiatry (YGAP) units began admitting patients in 2018. These units aim to provide standardized care to patients receiving psychiatric treatment under secure conditions, in suitable physical environments and in accordance with scientific principles and accepted clinical approaches. Inpatient treatment may end only when the individual no longer poses a danger to society. Outpatient follow-up is also subject to court supervision.
This study aimed to identify the temperament and character traits of patients hospitalized in a forensic psychiatry unit and compare them with those of psychiatric outpatients with similar disorders and a control group.
Methods
This cross-sectional study was conducted between December 1, 2024, and February 28, 2025, in the High-Security Forensic Psychiatry Unit and the Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of Adana City Training and Research Hospital. The patient groups comprised inpatients in the YGAP unit and psychiatric outpatients diagnosed with schizophrenia or bipolar affective disorder. Healthy volunteers constituted the control group. The study was designed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Adana City Training and Research Hospital before recruitment (November 7, 2024; approval no. 236). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients who did not provide written consent or lacked the mental capacity to complete the assessments were excluded.
The control group comprised healthy volunteers recruited from auxiliary healthcare personnel working in the YGAP unit and their relatives, matched to the patient groups in age and sex. The study included 66 YGAP inpatients, 25 psychiatric outpatients, and 24 healthy controls. All YGAP patients had been referred by the courts because of psychiatric illness in connection with offenses such as homicide, assault, theft, or damage to public or private property. Eligible patients were male and literate, had no severe hearing or visual impairment, and had neither cognitive impairment nor intellectual disability that would prevent them from understanding and completing the questionnaire. Outpatients were selected from male patients with schizophrenia or bipolar affective disorder who had been followed at the clinic for a long period and met the same eligibility criteria. Healthy controls were men of similar age and educational level, recruited from healthcare support staff, including cleaning and auxiliary personnel.
Sociodemographic data for patients and controls were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire. Temperament traits were assessed with the Turkish version of the TEMPS-A.
Sociodemographic questionnaire
The patient questionnaire recorded age, education, marital status, employment before hospitalization, social security coverage, and psychiatric diagnosis. The control questionnaire recorded age, education, marital status, and employment.
Temperament assessment
The Turkish version of the TEMPS-A comprises 99 items designed to assess depressive, hyperthymic, irritable, cyclothymic, and anxious temperaments. Respondents answer each item as yes or no while considering their experiences throughout their lives. The subscales comprise depressive (18 items), cyclothymic (19 items), hyperthymic (20 items), irritable (18 items), and anxious (24 items) temperament. The respective cutoff scores are 13, 18, 20, 13, and 18. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were evaluated by Vahip et al. (2005).
Statistical analysis
Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Because all continuous variables were normally distributed, analysis of variance was used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and counts (n). The threshold for statistical significance was set at 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0.
Results
A total of 115 participants were included. Mean age was 39.31 ± 10.67 years in the YGAP group (n = 66), 39.72 ± 8.32 years in the outpatient group (n = 25), and 37.0 ± 7.27 years in the control group (n = 24), with no significant difference between groups (p > 0.05). In the YGAP group, 29.4% had paranoid schizophrenia, 51.5% had other schizophrenia subtypes or atypical psychosis, and 19.1% had bipolar affective disorder. The corresponding proportions in the outpatient group were 18.4%, 52.3%, and 29.4%. The difference in educational attainment between groups was described as small but statistically significant (χ² = 13.72, p = 0.033).
The educational difference was attributed to a higher proportion of high-school graduates among controls, whereas most YGAP patients and outpatients had primary or middle-school education. Significant differences were also found between patient groups and controls in marital status, employment, and social security coverage. The proportion of unmarried participants was 66.68% in the YGAP group, 52% in the outpatient group, and 12.5% in the control group (χ² = 30.88, p < 0.000). Employment and social security coverage also differed significantly (χ² = 15.2, p < 0.000, and χ² = 17.18, p < 0.000, respectively). All controls were employed and had social security coverage. In contrast, 62.12% of YGAP patients and 52% of outpatients were employed; 53.03% and 60%, respectively, had social security coverage. Sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.
One-way ANOVA revealed significant between-group differences in the depressive, cyclothymic, irritable, and anxious TEMPS-A subscales (F = 32.126, p < 0.000; F = 16.126, p < 0.000; F = 44.248, p < 0.000; and F = 24.966, p < 0.000, respectively). Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test. The YGAP and outpatient groups did not differ significantly in cyclothymic, hyperthymic, or anxious temperament. Differences were significant for depressive and irritable temperament (SE = 0.951, p = 0.020; SE = 0.978, p = 0.002, respectively). Depressive temperament scores were higher in the outpatient group, whereas irritable temperament scores were higher in the YGAP group. When each patient group was compared separately with controls, significant differences were found in all subscales except hyperthymic temperament (Table 2).
Discussion
In this study of temperament and character traits among forensic psychiatric inpatients, YGAP patients differed from patients with similar characteristics but no history of offending in irritable and depressive temperament. Comparisons between the patient groups and healthy controls showed differences in all temperament traits except hyperthymic temperament.
Numerous studies have reported that irritable temperament traits are more pronounced in patients with psychotic symptoms (Kesebir, 2005; Cassano, 1989). Although our findings do not establish whether innate traits trigger offending behavior, they suggest that temperament and character may influence the course of illness. Irritable temperament may act as a trigger for offending. Uncontrolled anger may lead individuals toward behaviors ranging from assault to homicide. Reduced self-control has been described in patients with psychosis (Tomaz, 2023; Pompili, 2017).
Depressive temperament scores were significantly higher in YGAP patients than in outpatients. Both patient groups had experienced psychotic disorders since a young age. Long-term antipsychotic treatment may have contributed to increased depressive features (Reynolds and McGowan). In addition, court-ordered admission and the knowledge that discharge required court approval even after clinical improvement may have contributed to depressive temperament traits among YGAP patients.
When YGAP patients and outpatients were compared with controls, all temperament dimensions except hyperthymic temperament differed significantly. Hyperthymic temperament has been associated particularly with bipolar disorder (Placidi et al., 1998; Vahip et al., 2005). Our patient groups consisted predominantly of individuals with paranoid schizophrenia or other psychotic disorders (80.9% of YGAP patients and 70.7% of outpatients). The absence of a difference in hyperthymic temperament may be attributable to the small number of participants with bipolar affective disorder. It has been reported that switching to mania in bipolar disorder is not solely medication-related and that hyperthymic temperament is also an important risk factor influencing the course of illness (Kesebir, 2005).
Limitations include the relatively small patient groups. Approximately 90 patients were hospitalized in the YGAP unit during the study period; some were excluded because intellectual disability or severe cognitive impairment associated with psychosis prevented them from understanding the questionnaire. Another limitation is that the instrument used is more commonly administered in bipolar populations. Nevertheless, this study provides information on temperament traits in a sample composed predominantly of patients with psychotic disorders.
In conclusion, the significantly higher irritable temperament scores in the YGAP group than in the outpatient group led us to consider irritable temperament a potentially important factor in these patients’ involvement in various offenses.
