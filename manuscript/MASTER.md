## Abstract

**Objective:** To compare affective temperament profiles among high-security forensic psychiatric inpatients, psychiatric outpatients and healthy controls.

**Methods:** This cross-sectional study included 66 forensic psychiatric inpatients, 25 psychiatric outpatients and 24 healthy controls at Adana City Training and Research Hospital. Sociodemographic characteristics were recorded using a semi-structured form. Affective temperament was assessed with the Turkish version of the Temperament Evaluation of Memphis, Pisa, Paris and San Diego Autoquestionnaire (TEMPS-A). Group differences were examined using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey comparisons; Welch analyses and Games–Howell comparisons were examined as sensitivity analyses.

**Results:** Mean irritable temperament scores were 11.58 ± 4.78 in forensic inpatients, 8.12 ± 4.00 in outpatients and 2.29 ± 1.83 in controls. The forensic–outpatient mean difference was 3.46 points (Games–Howell 95% CI 1.06–5.85; p = .003). Depressive temperament scores were lower in forensic inpatients than in outpatients (10.09 ± 4.27 versus 12.68 ± 4.29); this comparison was significant within the dimension but did not remain significant in an exploratory correction across five temperament dimensions. No statistically significant forensic–outpatient differences were found for cyclothymic, hyperthymic or anxious temperament. Both patient groups scored higher than controls on all dimensions except hyperthymic temperament.

**Conclusion:** Higher irritable temperament scores were associated with forensic inpatient group membership in this sample. The design cannot distinguish associations with forensic status from those with treatment setting or other clinical and social differences, and does not establish causation or prediction of offending.

## Introduction

Violence risk in people with psychotic disorders varies across individuals and settings. Population-level evidence indicates an association between schizophrenia-spectrum disorders and violence perpetration, while longitudinal research identifies previous offending and substance misuse as important risk markers. These findings support considering clinical and social context when examining potential individual differences (Whiting et al., 2022; Lagerberg et al., 2025).

Affective temperament describes dimensions of characteristic emotional experience and response. TEMPS-A assesses depressive, cyclothymic, hyperthymic, irritable and anxious temperament. These dimensions should be distinguished from current psychiatric symptoms and from character constructs measured by other instruments (Vahip et al., 2005).

Research comparing patients with schizophrenia with healthy controls has identified differences in several affective temperament dimensions (Dib et al., 2021). Temperament has also been examined in relation to aggression in bipolar disorder (Fico et al., 2023). These findings do not establish that temperament contributes independently to offending, and comparisons involving forensic psychiatric populations warrant attention to diagnostic composition and treatment setting.

This study aimed to compare affective temperament profiles among high-security forensic psychiatric inpatients, psychiatric outpatients and healthy controls. The comparison describes group differences rather than prospectively assessing violence or reoffending.

### Design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between December 1, 2024, and February 28, 2025, in the High-Security Forensic Psychiatry (YGAP) Unit and Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of Adana City Training and Research Hospital. Ethics approval was obtained on November 7, 2024 (approval no. 236). The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and participants provided their own written informed consent. ### Participants

The sample comprised 66 forensic psychiatric inpatients, 25 psychiatric outpatients and 24 healthy controls. The source manuscript describes all participants as male. Forensic inpatients had been referred by courts in connection with offenses including homicide, assault, theft and property damage. These categories include both violent and nonviolent offenses. Offense information was reviewed from forensic records, according to the author; these documents were not supplied for this computational audit.

Patients were required to be literate and able to understand and complete the questionnaire. Severe hearing or visual impairment, or cognitive impairment or intellectual disability preventing questionnaire completion, were reported as exclusion criteria. Declining written informed consent was also an exclusion criterion. The ability to complete questionnaires should be distinguished from capacity to provide informed consent.

Outpatients were recruited from patients receiving ongoing psychiatric care. Diagnoses were based on existing clinical records and assessment by the supervising psychiatrist. Patients were considered clinically stable on the supervising psychiatrist’s clinical assessment. ### Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as means and standard deviations; categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. The archived SPSS syntax includes distributional assessments, Levene tests, classical and Welch one-way ANOVA, Tukey and Games–Howell comparisons, chi-square tests and Kruskal–Wallis analyses. Several group distributions showed departures from normality in the archived tests; the original statement that all continuous variables were normally distributed was not supported. Classical results were independently reproduced in Python. Welch/Games–Howell results are retained as sensitivity analyses because several variables show unequal variances. Confidence intervals for Games–Howell comparisons use the studentized-range distribution for three groups.

An additional exploratory Holm correction was applied across the five forensic–outpatient temperament comparisons to examine sensitivity to multiplicity across dimensions. This was introduced during manuscript review and was not prespecified. Categorical Pearson statistics are reproduced for audit purposes; inference for sparse tables remains provisional. Missing diagnoses in controls were not imputed. All records and original category codes were retained.

As an exploratory addition during manuscript review, forensic–outpatient standardized differences were calculated as Hedges’ g using a sample-size-weighted pooled SD and the exact small-sample correction. Pointwise 95% percentile bootstrap intervals used 20,000 within-group resamples of existing participants (seed 20260920). These intervals were not multiplicity-adjusted and do not replace the Holm analysis. Raw-score differences remain the principal estimates because a pooled-SD interpretation is limited by unequal variances.

## Results

### Sample characteristics

The sample comprised 115 participants. Mean ages were 39.32 ± 10.68 years in forensic inpatients, 39.72 ± 8.32 years in outpatients and 37.00 ± 7.27 years in controls. Classical ANOVA did not show a statistically significant age difference (F[2,112] = 0.626, p = .537); Welch analysis gave the same qualitative conclusion (p = .396).

Educational distributions differed descriptively: 33 of 66 forensic inpatients (50.0%) had middle-school education, whereas high-school education was the most frequent category in outpatients (13/25, 52.0%) and controls (14/24, 58.3%). The reproduced Pearson test was χ²(6) = 13.720, p = .033, but sparse expected counts require review before treating this as final inference. Marital status, employment and social-security distributions are provided in Table 1.

### Affective temperament

Classical ANOVA identified between-group differences in depressive, cyclothymic, irritable and anxious temperament scores (all p < .001), with no statistically significant difference for hyperthymic temperament (p = .081). Welch analyses produced the same qualitative pattern. Kruskal–Wallis sensitivity analyses also identified omnibus differences in the four nonhyperthymic dimensions (all p < .001), but not hyperthymic temperament (p = .116); these rank-based findings do not establish the individual pairwise mean contrasts. Aggregate estimates and both test families are supplied in Table 2 and the analysis report.

Forensic inpatients had higher irritable temperament scores than outpatients: mean difference 3.46 points, Tukey p = .002; Games–Howell p = .003, 95% CI 1.06–5.85. Depressive scores were lower in forensic inpatients: mean difference −2.59 points, Tukey p = .020; Games–Howell p = .036, 95% CI −5.03 to −0.15. Differences in cyclothymic, hyperthymic and anxious scores were not statistically significant between these patient groups.

In the exploratory Holm sensitivity across five focal temperament comparisons, the irritable comparison remained significant (p = .015), whereas the depressive comparison did not (p = .142). Both patient groups scored higher than controls on depressive, cyclothymic, irritable and anxious dimensions in the within-dimension comparisons. Absence of a significant hyperthymic difference does not demonstrate equivalence.

Exploratory standardized forensic–outpatient differences were g = 0.748 for irritable temperament (pointwise bootstrap 95% CI 0.320–1.261) and g = −0.600 for depressive temperament (−1.091 to −0.156). Intervals are not corrected across dimensions; the depressive interval therefore does not overturn the nonsignificant Holm result. All five estimates are provided in [Supplementary Table S1](../analysis/generated/EFFECT_SIZES.md).

## Discussion

The principal finding was a higher mean irritable temperament score in forensic psychiatric inpatients than in psychiatric outpatients. This association persisted under unequal-variance comparisons and an exploratory adjustment across five temperament dimensions. Depressive scores were higher in outpatients, although this finding was sensitive to the scope of multiplicity correction. The three-group design places the forensic–outpatient contrast alongside the broader patient–control pattern: four temperament dimensions differed from controls, but irritability was the clearest distinction between the patient groups after the exploratory cross-dimension correction. This is a descriptive contribution, not evidence of a forensic-specific temperament profile. These observations do not establish that temperament preceded or caused offending.

The patient–control differences can be compared with the TEMPS-A study in schizophrenia by Dib et al. (2021), while work in bipolar disorder provides a separate context for temperament and aggression (Fico et al., 2023). These populations and outcomes are not interchangeable. A Turkish study by Çatlı et al. (2026) reported no significant temperament differences between offender and non-offender schizophrenia groups. That study recruited forensic and general psychiatric outpatients, whereas the present forensic group comprised inpatients. This difference in setting limits direct comparison and reinforces the need to distinguish forensic status from hospitalization. Dib et al. reported higher hyperthymic scores in controls than in patients, whereas our omnibus hyperthymic comparison was not significant. These mixed findings should be acknowledged rather than described as uniform replication.

Forensic inpatient status incorporates both legal circumstances and hospitalization. Without a general psychiatric inpatient comparison group, associations with these components cannot be separated. Diagnostic composition, symptom burden, substance use, treatment exposure and social circumstances could also contribute to observed differences; the current dataset does not support comprehensive adjustment for these factors.

Depressive temperament is distinct from a current depressive episode or depressive symptoms. The data therefore do not support attributing the observed score difference to antipsychotic effects or court-ordered admission. Similarly, the absence of a hyperthymic group difference cannot presently be explained by the proportion of participants with bipolar disorder because diagnosis coding remains unresolved.

### Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes temporal or causal conclusions. The sample was relatively small, unequal across groups and restricted to men at one center. Recruitment of controls from hospital employees limits representativeness and may help explain employment differences. The exact sampling and matching procedures remain to be specified. Screening by record review and self-report may not exclude all psychiatric conditions. Self-reported temperament may be influenced by current symptoms; stability was judged clinically, and standardized symptom measurements are not available in the supplied dataset. Mixed self-completion and reading assistance may affect response comparability. Multiple dimensions and comparisons increase the risk of chance findings. Offense severity and prospective violence were not measured as outcomes in the available dataset. Item-level TEMPS-A responses were not provided for computational verification of scoring or internal consistency; local verification by the research team remains necessary.

## Conclusion

In this sample, forensic psychiatric inpatients had higher irritable temperament scores than psychiatric outpatients. Interpretation of the depressive temperament difference was sensitive to multiplicity correction. These findings support further study of affective temperament across psychiatric settings but do not establish temperament as a cause or predictor of offending.
